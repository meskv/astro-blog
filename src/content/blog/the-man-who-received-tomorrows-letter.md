## The first letter
The first letter arrived on a Tuesday.
There was nothing unusual about the envelope.
No stamp. No name. No return address.
It had simply been placed beneath the door of Room 17 sometime during the night.
Inside was a single sheet of paper.
It said:
> At 4:17 this afternoon, the glass in the kitchen window will break.
Nothing else.
Arun laughed when he read it.
He lived alone on the third floor of an old apartment building, and the kitchen window had been there longer than he had.
At 4:17, nothing happened.
4:18.
4:19.
He smiled.
Then, at 4:23, he heard a crash.
A football had come through the kitchen window.
Arun stood completely still.
It wasn't the prediction that frightened him.
It was the fact that the football had landed exactly where the letter had said the glass would fall.
## The second letter
The next morning, another envelope waited beneath his door.
This time, the message was longer.
> You will forget your umbrella.
	You will notice it at 8:31.
	You will go back for it.
	On your way back, you will meet a woman wearing a yellow coat.
Arun read it twice.
Then he put the letter in his pocket and deliberately left his umbrella beside the door.
At 8:31, halfway down the stairs, he stopped.
He had forgotten it.
He went back upstairs.
And there she was.
A woman in a yellow coat was standing outside Room 17.
She looked at him.
He looked at her.
Neither spoke.
Then she said:
> “You got one too.”
## The room upstairs
Her name was Mira.
She had received letters for three weeks.
Every letter predicted something small.
A broken cup.
A missed train.
A phone call from someone she hadn't spoken to in years.
Nothing important.
Nothing impossible.
Until the previous morning.
Her letter had said:
> Tomorrow, you will meet the man who has been receiving these letters.
Arun looked at her.
“Why us?”
Mira shook her head.
“I don't know.”
Then she pulled another envelope from her bag.
“This arrived this morning.”
Arun opened it.
There was only one sentence.
> You have exactly seven days.
## The equation
They began looking for patterns.
They wrote down every prediction.
Date.
Time.
Event.
Location.
After several hours, Mira noticed something strange.
The letters seemed to become more frequent.
At first, there had been one every seven days.
Then every three days.
Then every day.
Then twice a day.
She wrote an equation on the wall:
$$
T_n = T_0 - n\Delta t
$$
Arun stared at it.
“What does that mean?”
“If the time between letters keeps shrinking,” she said, “eventually there won't be any time left between them.”
“How much time?”
Mira calculated.
Then she stopped.
“Seven days.”
“The letter said seven days.”
“No.”
She looked at him.
“It said we have seven days.”
She pointed at the equation.
“The letters aren't predicting the future.”
“They're counting down to something.”
## The last envelope
For the next six days, nothing happened.
No letters.
No strange events.
No woman in yellow.
No broken windows.
On the seventh morning, Arun woke up before sunrise.
There was an envelope beneath his door.
This one was different.
It had his name written on it.
Not printed.
Handwritten.
He opened it.
Inside was a single sentence.
> If you are reading this, you have already made the wrong choice.
Arun felt cold.
Mira called him.
Her voice was shaking.
“Arun.”
“What?”
“I found something.”
“What?”
“The letters.”
“What about them?”
“They aren't coming from the future.”
There was a pause.
“Then where are they coming from?”
Mira whispered:
> “Us.”
## Room 17
They returned to the apartment.
The room was empty.
Almost.
Behind the old wardrobe, they found a narrow wooden door.
Neither of them had noticed it before.
There was no handle.
Only a small slot.
The same size as the envelopes.
Arun looked at Mira.
Mira looked at him.
Then they heard something from the other side.
Three knocks.
Slow.
Deliberate.
Knock.
Knock.
Knock.
Arun reached for the door.
Before he could touch it, an envelope slid through the slot.
Mira picked it up.
She opened it.
There were only two words.
> Don't open.
They stood silently in the room.
Then Arun laughed.
Not because anything was funny.
But because he suddenly understood the trick.
The letters had never been telling them what would happen.
They had been telling them what they would do.
And now there was only one question left.
Would they obey the final letter?
Or would they write the next one themselves?
