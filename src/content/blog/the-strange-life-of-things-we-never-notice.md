There is a strange habit that human beings have.
We spend most of our lives surrounded by things we barely see.
The chair we sit on every morning. The road outside our window. The tree that has been standing at the same corner for twenty years. The old building we pass every day without wondering who lived there before us.
We see these things.
But we rarely *notice* them.
And perhaps there is a difference between seeing something and noticing it.
## The world does not become interesting when we travel
We often imagine that interesting things happen somewhere else.
A different city.
A different country.
A mountain we have never climbed.
A street we have never walked through.
A person we have never met.
We save photographs of places we want to visit while barely remembering what the sky looked like this morning.
There is something slightly absurd about this.
We can spend three hours watching a documentary about an ancient civilization and then walk past a hundred-year-old building without wondering who built it.
We can become fascinated by the life of a stranger on the other side of the planet while knowing almost nothing about the person who lives two doors away.
Perhaps the problem is not that our lives are ordinary.
Perhaps the problem is that we have become very good at ignoring the ordinary.

## Imagine if objects could remember
Consider a wooden table.
To you, it is a table.
But imagine that the table could remember.
It might remember the first person who touched it.
The first meal eaten on it.
The argument that happened beside it.
The child who once fell asleep with his head resting against its surface.
The cup that broke on a rainy afternoon.
The letter that someone wrote but never sent.
The table would not be a piece of furniture anymore.
It would be an archive.
And perhaps everything around us is like that.
We simply don't have access to the archive.
## The mathematics of an ordinary day
There is another strange thing about everyday life.
Small events appear insignificant individually, but they accumulate.
Suppose an ordinary day contains just 20 moments that you genuinely remember.
After a year, that would give us:
$$
20 \times 365 = 7300
$$
Seven thousand three hundred remembered moments.
Of course, real memory does not work like a spreadsheet.
We forget most things.
Some moments disappear within seconds. Others remain for decades for reasons we don't understand.
A smell can bring back a childhood afternoon.
A particular song can return you to a room you haven't entered in fifteen years.
A sentence spoken by someone you barely remember can suddenly become important.
Memory is not a perfect recording.
It is more like a strange editor.
It decides what deserves another viewing.
## We underestimate small changes
A similar thing happens with our lives.
We often expect change to arrive dramatically.
We imagine a great decision.
A sudden opportunity.
A completely new beginning.
But most changes are much quieter.
Reading ten pages today.
Walking for twenty minutes.
Calling someone you haven't spoken to in months.
Writing one paragraph.
Learning one idea.
Doing something badly and then doing it slightly better tomorrow.
The individual change is almost invisible.
But if a small improvement is repeated, the effect becomes noticeable.
We can describe this very simply:
$$
A_n = A_0(1+r)^n
$$
The equation is usually used to describe growth.
But there is something almost philosophical about it.
A small difference repeated many times does not remain small.
## The things we walk past

*Image: Unsplash*
I sometimes wonder about old buildings.
Not beautiful buildings.
Ordinary ones.
The buildings with faded paint, broken balconies and wires running across their walls.
How many lives have passed through them?
Someone was born there.
Someone celebrated a birthday there.
Someone cried in one of those rooms.
Someone probably looked through one of those windows and wondered what their life would become.
Then decades passed.
People moved away.
Children grew old.
Names disappeared from doors.
And the building remained.
There is something humbling about that.
We experience our lives as though they are the centre of everything.
But the world has an enormous capacity to continue without us.
## Perhaps attention is a form of gratitude
This is where I think noticing becomes important.
Noticing does not mean becoming fascinated by everything.
It simply means occasionally allowing something ordinary to become visible.
Look at the tree outside your window.
Really look at it.
It has probably changed more than you have noticed.
Look at the road you walk on every day.
There are cracks in it that weren't there five years ago.
Look at an old photograph.
There are people in it whose names you have forgotten.
And yet, for a brief moment, they were completely real.

Maybe attention is one of the simplest forms of gratitude.
Not because everything is beautiful.
It isn't.
But because everything is temporary.
## The unnoticed ending
There is another thought that makes this strangely uncomfortable.
We rarely know when something is happening for the last time.
The last time you sit in a particular classroom.
The last conversation with an old friend.
The last evening in a childhood home.
The last time someone calls you by a nickname.
The last walk down a particular street.
There is almost never a bell.
Nothing announces:
> This is the last time.
Life simply continues.
And then, much later, memory quietly informs us that something ended without asking our permission.
Perhaps this is why ordinary moments sometimes become precious only after they are gone.
## Maybe the ordinary is enough
We spend an enormous amount of energy trying to make our lives extraordinary.
There is nothing wrong with ambition.
There is nothing wrong with wanting to build something meaningful.
But perhaps a meaningful life does not require every moment to be meaningful.
Perhaps it is enough to have a few things you care about deeply.
A few people you love.
A few questions you never stop asking.
A little work that matters to you.
And enough attention to occasionally notice the strange, beautiful fact that you are here at all.
The world is full of things that don't announce themselves.
A tree growing silently.
A letter sitting unread.
A building holding decades of memories.
A person sitting beside you who may be thinking something you will never know.
An ordinary afternoon that, years from now, you might desperately wish you could experience once more.
We keep looking for extraordinary lives.
Perhaps we should sometimes look again at ordinary ones.
They may have been extraordinary all along.
